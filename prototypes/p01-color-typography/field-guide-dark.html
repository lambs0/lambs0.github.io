Pacific Northwest · Field notes

Life along the tide line

A starter guide to a few familiar animals of the Pacific Northwest coast. Watch wildlife from a respectful distance, and leave the shore as you found it.

Dark color version of the light marine field guide.

Meet the coastal wildlife

Orca

Orcas are toothed whales in the dolphin family. In the Pacific Northwest, different communities have distinct diets and social traditions. Look for their tall dorsal fins from shore or a boat, and give them plenty of space.

Lifespan
Males average about 30 years; females average about 50 years. Some live much longer.
Weight
Adult males average about 7,200 kg; females are generally smaller.
Diet
Resident orcas mainly eat salmon; Bigg’s orcas mainly eat marine mammals.

Sources: NOAA Fisheries and Animal Diversity Web.

Sea otter

Sea otters use dense fur to stay warm and often float at the surface while grooming or resting. Along the outer coast, they are an important part of nearshore kelp-forest life.

Lifespan
Estimated maximum in the wild: about 23 years.
Weight
Adult females are about 50–70 lb; males are about 80–100 lb.
Diet
Sea urchins, crabs, clams, mussels, octopus, fish, and other invertebrates.

Sources: Alaska Department of Fish and Game and Animal Diversity Web.

Harbor seal

Harbor seals haul out on beaches and rocks to rest. If one is resting on shore, observe quietly from far away; approaching can cause stress or send it back into the water.

Lifespan
Wild-lifespan estimates vary; one reference estimates an average of about 40 years.
Weight
Adults can weigh up to about 285 lb; size varies by sex and region.
Diet
Mainly fish, shellfish, and crustaceans.

Sources: NOAA Fisheries and Animal Diversity Web.

Harbor porpoise

These small, shy cetaceans surface briefly in coastal waters. Their low, triangular dorsal fins may appear and disappear quickly, so watch patiently from shore and avoid following them by boat.

Lifespan
Estimates range from about 6 to 20 years.
Weight
About 45–65 kg (100–143 lb).
Diet
Mostly small fish; squid and other cephalopods are also eaten.

Sources: NOAA Fisheries and Animal Diversity Web.

Gray whale

Gray whales migrate along the Pacific coast between feeding and breeding areas. During migration, a whale may be spotted from a headland or beach; use binoculars rather than approaching it.

Lifespan
Estimates vary; reported estimates range from about 25 to 80 years.
Weight
Adults can weigh about 90,000 lb.
Diet
Mostly small seafloor invertebrates, including amphipods.

Sources: NOAA Fisheries and Animal Diversity Web.

Ochre sea star

This colorful tide-pool resident clings to rocks with tiny tube feet. Tide pools are delicate habitats, so watch without lifting animals or moving rocks.

Lifespan
May live up to about 20 years.
Size
About 25–45 cm (10–18 in) across. Weight is not commonly listed for this species.
Diet
Mussels, limpets, chitons, snails, barnacles, and other small marine animals.

Source: Animal Diversity Web.

Dungeness crab

This broad-shelled crab lives on sandy or muddy seafloors, including bays and estuaries. It scavenges and hunts along the bottom.

Lifespan
Estimated maximum: about 8–13 years.
Weight
A legal-sized male is about 6.5 in across the shell and weighs around 2 lb.
Diet
Clams, worms, shrimp, fish, and dead animals it finds on the seafloor.

Source: Alaska Department of Fish and Game.

Giant green anemone

This tide-pool animal anchors itself to rocky shores with a ring of tentacles around its mouth. Its bright green color comes partly from algae living in its tissues.

Lifespan
Wild lifespan is estimated at up to about 150 years, though information is limited.
Size
Its tentacle crown can reach about 25 cm across; its body can grow to about 30 cm tall.
Diet
Sea urchins, mussels, crabs, and small fish.

Source: Animal Diversity Web.
